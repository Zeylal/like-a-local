*Where history, food and everyday life somehow all happen on the same street.*

Turkey is a place I don't really know how to experience from a checklist.

Part of it is personal. I'm Turkish, but I've also spent much of my life outside Turkey, so traveling here feels a little different. I'm constantly switching between "I know this" and "wait, I've never noticed this before."

I come for the food, obviously. But also for the markets, the old neighborhoods, the chaotic streets, the tea glasses, the conversations that last longer than planned, and the feeling that there is always another layer of the city hiding behind the obvious one.

I want to see the famous places, but I also want to know where people actually go when they're not sightseeing.

---

## Istanbul

*Two continents, approximately 47 personalities.*

Istanbul is impossible to describe as just one city.

One minute you're standing inside Hagia Sophia, surrounded by centuries of Byzantine and Ottoman history. The next you're drinking tea in a neighborhood café, crossing the Bosphorus, wandering through a market or getting completely lost in a side street.

I want to experience both versions.

The monumental Istanbul of Hagia Sophia, the Blue Mosque, Topkapı Palace and the Basilica Cistern, but also the everyday Istanbul of Balat, Cihangir, Ortaköy and the Spice Bazaar. The places where people aren't necessarily there to "see Istanbul", but simply to live in it.

### What I'm looking for

**History** — Byzantine Istanbul, Ottoman Istanbul and everything that came between them.

**Food** — Markets, kebabs, sweets, tea, street food and whatever smells too good to walk past.

**Neighborhood life** — Cafés, little shops, galleries, streets full of people and the places that don't necessarily make the postcard.

**The Bosphorus** — Because apparently one city sitting between two continents wasn't dramatic enough.

## Antalya

*More than beaches.*

Antalya is where the Mediterranean meets thousands of years of history.

Yes, there are turquoise beaches and dramatic cliffs. But what makes it interesting to me is how close the ancient world is to everyday life.

You can walk through Kaleiçi, with its Ottoman houses and narrow streets, and then end up by the sea. You can visit Hadrian's Gate, drive out to ancient Perge or Aspendos, and later be eating dinner somewhere in the old town.

And then there are the beaches.

Konyaaltı, Lara, Kaputaş, Phaselis and the coast around Kaş all offer completely different ways of experiencing the Mediterranean. Kekova is the one I keep coming back to — you can swim or take a boat over submerged ancient ruins.

### The Antalya I want

Not just resort Antalya.

I want old Antalya, ancient Antalya, local Antalya, seaside Antalya.

And obviously, I want to eat my way through it.

## İzmir

*A different kind of Turkey.*

İzmir feels more relaxed.

The sea is always somewhere nearby, people linger outside, and the city has a younger, more Mediterranean rhythm.

I'd spend time around Kordon, wander through Kemeraltı Bazaar, climb up to Asansör, and spend an evening around Gül Sokak.

And then there's Alaçatı, with its stone houses, cafés and slower holiday atmosphere.

But the biggest reason to come here is just outside the city:

**Ephesus.**

One of the places where history becomes almost ridiculous in scale.

You can walk through an ancient city and suddenly realize that the streets you're standing on were already busy thousands of years ago.

## Food

*The actual reason I travel.*

Turkey has an unfair amount of food.

And apparently we decided one word wasn't enough, so we have approximately 100 different versions of kebab.

Adana. Urfa. Döner. İskender. Beyti. Ali Nazik. Testi kebab. Çöp şiş. Kuzu tandır...

Calling everything "kebab" is basically culinary laziness.

But food isn't just restaurants. It's:

- çay in tiny glasses
- fresh bread
- spices from the market
- lokum
- a breakfast that somehow contains an entire dairy section
- eating something from a place because everyone around you is eating it

And then saying "I'm full" before ordering something else.

## What I bring home

I don't really want generic souvenirs.

I like things that feel like they belong to the place: Turkish tea, spices, lokum, ceramics and tableware — particularly the colorful handmade pieces associated with Cappadocia.

For me, that's the kind of thing that makes a trip continue after you've come home.

A little ceramic object on a shelf.

A packet of spice in the kitchen.

A tea glass.

Something handmade.

Something that makes you look at it six months later and remember exactly where you bought it.

---

## My way of traveling Turkey

I don't want to see everything.

I want to notice something.

A grandmother buying bread.

A crowded tea garden.

A tiny restaurant with no English menu.

The way people spend an evening by the water.

A shopkeeper who insists you try something.

A neighborhood that wasn't in our itinerary.

A meal that becomes the best thing we ate all week.

Because that's the Turkey I'm interested in.

Not just the Turkey you photograph. The Turkey people actually live in.
